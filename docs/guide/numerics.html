Numerical methods

State and coordinates

The physical state is a history of accepted localized sources in a homogeneous fluid. Each source retains its position, start time, wavelength parameter, pressure strength, and forcing mode. The field is the sum of their responses at the requested physical time: radial responses in open water, weighted radial images for partial reflections, or cosine modes between ideal reflecting walls.

The observation grid samples that continuous response. It is not a finite difference state that is advanced once per animation frame. Changing the grid resolution resamples the same sources at the same time. Existing waves therefore retain their phase and wavelength. Coarsening the grid can still reduce visible detail; it cannot make unresolved wavelengths accurate.

Mouse clicks and rain place sources at the nearest observation-grid node. Changing resolution changes the spacing available to later interactive inputs; accepted source positions remain fixed. Programmatic addRipple calls retain their supplied physical coordinates.

Positions, wavelengths, and surface elevation use meters. Time uses seconds. The renderer normalizes horizontal coordinates by the domain width and applies the selected vertical exaggeration to elevation. Neither transformation enters the physical calculation.

Automatic height scaling compares the peak center displacement of a unit Gaussian pressure impulse with the corresponding response in water at 20 °C. Both calculations use the selected characteristic wavelength and the full viscous response. The water reference has a display multiplier of 100; the selected fluid uses 100 times the ratio of the two reference peaks, rounded to three significant digits and bounded between 1 and 1,000,000. The impulse reference is also used in wavemaker mode. This is a visibility convention, not a physical amplitude correction.

The reference calculation occurs when the fluid properties or wavelength change. Up to sixteen results are retained for reuse. Playback applies the fixed multiplier to the existing surface; no amplitude search or rescaling based on the current wave field occurs per frame. Pressure strength is excluded from the ratio, so changing it still changes the visible displacement.

Elapsed-time playback

The playback clock measures elapsed time independently of the rendering rate. Each frame samples the wave field at the current simulation time. If rendering takes longer on a particular computer, fewer intermediate views are shown; the waves do not propagate more slowly. Elapsed playback time excludes user pauses and input preparation through the first complete frame. A playback-rate change applies only to subsequent elapsed time.

Scheduled rain retains each drop's due time even when an update arrives late. The response is evaluated at its resulting age, preserving the same source history and wave field at matching simulation times. Frame-rate measurement uses a separate real-time clock and does not drive propagation.

Here J₀ is the Bessel function of the first kind, order zero, and hε is the inverse Laplace transform of the viscous transfer function. The positive pressure strength J acts downward. Elevation, vertical velocity, radial slope, and their mixed derivative follow from the same integral.

The viscous temporal response contains a pair of surface-wave poles and a branch contribution associated with diffusing vorticity. A paired pole representation remains finite when the poles approach one another near the transition to nonoscillatory relaxation. Dropping the branch contribution would change the initial transient and the late response.

The pole pair is calculated in batches through a scaled factorization of its quartic equation. Computing the slow pole from the pole product avoids cancellation in strongly viscous modes. This evaluates the same transfer function without a general polynomial-root calculation for every wavenumber. The temporal pole terms are evaluated in the same bounded modal blocks as the branch integral. Real sine and cosine factors evaluate conjugate pairs; local series preserve accuracy as the poles coalesce. For wavemaker excitation, preparation combines the branch-quadrature weights for each forcing frequency before evaluating its sine and cosine terms. Elevation and velocity share one matrix contraction of the exponential terms, with separate weights for the response and its time derivative. Conjugate symmetry gives the oscillatory pole-pair response from one complex convolution. Independent modal convolutions are evaluated in bounded arrays. After the forcing ends, each pole contribution is propagated from its value at that final forcing time. Near coalescence, a divided-difference formulation retains numerical accuracy. These evaluations preserve the prescribed forcing integral.

The real-axis radial and reflecting cosine-mode calculations use 64 quadrature points for the viscous branch. Checks against 256 points cover weakly and strongly viscous modes, the transition between oscillation and relaxation, and both source types. These quadrature settings affect numerical accuracy, not the physical damping rate.

At early ages, composite Gaussian quadrature evaluates the real-wavenumber integral. The quadrature density increases with the oscillation in space and time. Large quadratures use the piecewise Chebyshev interpolation described under reflecting-wall reconstruction to evaluate the temporal response. Every original quadrature node contributes to the spatial reconstruction. Across those sampled cases, the largest difference in any of the four fields is below 2.5 × 10⁻⁹ of that field's peak absolute reference value. This measures interpolation error separately from the quadrature error.

At sufficiently late ages, a qualified low-wavenumber contour avoids cancellation in an otherwise highly oscillatory integral. A real-axis calculation completes the higher-wavenumber part when viscosity requires a smaller contour region. The terminal arc and viscous branch are retained.

These quadrature methods evaluate the same wave response over its wavenumber spectrum.

Open-water response tables

Immutable response tables accelerate repeated evaluation. Their four stored fields support a tensor cubic Hermite interpolant in radius and time. Radial nodes are spaced at one thirty-second of the source wavelength parameter. Time nodes are denser near the viscous onset and during the early oscillations.

The app prepares the complete finite source lifetime when a source configuration is selected. During playback, open-water sources sample these tables; a new radial integral is not needed when a source becomes old. An input's finite-duration envelope reaches zero at the tabulated endpoint. Tables belonging to active sources remain pinned when source settings change.

Exact numerical equilibrium

Physical dissipation is already part of the viscous solution. The app also applies the finite-duration envelope: each source becomes exactly zero after ten settling time scales. The scale is the larger of its inviscid reference period and the longest retained Stokes relaxation time. That additional approximation is distinct from viscosity. Numerical omission estimates do not measure the envelope's difference from an indefinitely evolving viscous solution.

Each accepted source keeps its full precomputed response until that deadline. The envelope multiplies elevation and spatial gradients. Vertical velocity includes both the envelope-scaled physical velocity and the derivative of the envelope times physical elevation. This preserves consistency between the displayed displacement and its time derivative.

At the deadline, elevation, velocity, and spatial gradients are exactly zero for that source. Removing it then introduces no further approximation to the finite-duration model. Later sources retain their own start times and deadlines. Reflection does not restart a source's age or its settling envelope.

The unmodified viscous continuum response generally approaches equilibrium asymptotically. The app's exactly flat final surface is a deliberate consequence of the stated finite-duration approximation, not a new physical viscosity law.

The observation grid does not determine the modal spectrum. The time table normally uses 128 intervals per settling time scale. For larger modal spectra it uses 64 or 32 intervals to stay within the response-storage budget, retaining additional samples through the physical forcing interval and both Hermite fields. This changes interpolation accuracy, not the governing response or retained wavenumbers.

A bounded cache reuses immutable modal responses calculated per unit pressure strength. Responses share a cache entry when the fluid, domain, wavelength, excitation method, response duration, and memory budget agree. Each experiment retains its own source history, wave state, and clock. Existing sources keep their response tables when the source settings change; a new table uses the remaining storage budget.

Numerical evaluation and accuracy

Partial reflections require a domain between 6.5 and 26 characteristic wavelengths wide. The response is precomputed for the complete finite source lifetime. The image order is at most four in each direction and decreases for wider domains to keep the radial table within its memory limit; at the maximum width it is two. This is a numerical operating range, not a physical restriction on reflection.

Its distant image contributions therefore decrease more rapidly than those of the original response. The smooth part retains the broad, slowly relaxing contribution. The filtered infinite sum is evaluated in Fourier space using the summed geometric series of even and odd images. A change of integration variable resolves its narrow peaks when the reflection coefficient approaches one. The remaining finite sum has much smaller long-wave contributions. This is a numerical decomposition of the same weighted-image model; it adds no damping and does not change the viscosity or reflection coefficient.

Preparation separates the radial Hermite table and the filtered correction into shared spatial and temporal factors. An adaptive orthogonal projection reduces the matrix before its singular-value decomposition. The complete reconstruction is checked against the original samples; a full decomposition is used if the reduced calculation cannot meet the prescribed tolerance. The peak-scaled Frobenius residual must be at most 10⁻¹⁰ for the combined response and 10⁻¹² for the filtered spectral response. These are factorization tolerances, separate from quadrature, interpolation, and single-precision storage errors. A private deterministic random number stream makes the projection repeatable without changing the application's random state.

The weighted spatial images are then summed once. Their cubic-Hermite interpolation operators are combined in bounded blocks before multiplication by the spatial factors. This preserves the image weights while avoiding a separate large matrix product for every image. Transposed image groups share the same spatial data. With identical coefficients at all four walls, the even/even and odd/odd groups are symmetric under interchange of the horizontal coordinates. Each is assembled on one triangular half of the grid and completed by transposition. The mixed-parity group uses the full grid. This symmetry preserves every retained image and its coefficient. The smooth correction uses 17 spatial nodes per direction before interpolation onto the preparation grid. A new source translates this precomputed basis to the observation grid. Inputs at the same position reuse an active spatial mapping for that response and grid. Their start times and pressure strengths remain independent. A pressure strength change reuses the precomputed maps for scheduled sources; a change in wavelength or excitation method prepares new maps. The combined-wave loading check uses the requested pressure strength before the configuration is accepted. Within the animation-storage budget, each source's elevation history is added to the combined display sequence. Direct evaluation of the precomputed factors supplies onset corrections and the fallback when that budget is exceeded. Neither path traces reflection paths during playback. Precomputed spatial factors use single precision, and derivatives follow from the same interpolated elevation. The spatial preparation grid targets at least 24 intervals per characteristic wavelength. If that grid exceeds the storage budget, a smaller preparation grid retains at least 12 intervals per wavelength. Local fifth-degree interpolation translates its factors to the display grid. The selected display resolution and physical source definition remain unchanged.

Changing only the reflection coefficient reuses the most recent radial response and filtered spectral factors. Their storage is bounded at 128 MiB and 8 MiB, respectively. The coefficient-dependent image sums are recalculated for an unfamiliar configuration. Up to two complete partial-reflection responses are also retained within a 512 MiB budget, allowing a recent configuration to be selected again without rebuilding its image sums. Source histories, input maps, graphics, and playback clocks remain independently owned.

Finite residual-image truncation, Fourier quadrature, and interpolation introduce numerical error. They are distinct from the prescribed boundary approximation, which assumes a real, frequency-independent reflection coefficient. See Partial wave reflection for its physical meaning.

The following checks use the response at unit pressure strength before applying the common finite-duration envelope. An independent reconstruction checks elevations at all four walls, all four corners, and nearby interior points for an off-center source. The checked cases include water at a 50 mm source scale with R = 0.75, and a viscous reference fluid at 5 mm with R = 0.99 under both excitation methods. The reference properties are 1496 kg/m³, 7.85 Pa s, and 0.0801 N/m, based on measured honey properties (Tyowua et al., 2023). Refinement compares residual-image orders 4, 8, and 12 and Fourier quadrature orders 128 and 256. Across six sampled ages, the largest elevation difference is below 0.002% of each case's largest sampled reference elevation. Reflection and coordinate-exchange symmetries are also checked. The ideal-wall solution is checked separately against a direct cosine expansion and zero normal surface slope at every wall. These sampled checks assess the numerical reconstruction; they are not a universal error bound or an experimental validation of a wall.

A further 5 mm water case at R = 0.75 checks repeated returns. Restricting the sum to the first nine images, with |m|, |n| ≤ 1, changes the late sampled field by up to 11.2% of the largest reference elevation in that check. The complete implementation agrees with the refined reference within 0.00075% on the same observations and ages. These comparisons distinguish retained repeated reflections from the first returns alone.

Separate limiting-case checks compare coefficients of 0.000001 and 0.999999 with independently evaluated unbounded and ideal-wall fields. For the tested water and viscous-reference cases at a 50 mm source scale, the largest differences are below 0.001% of the corresponding peak reference elevation.

Spatial sampling

The surface grid provides the spatial discretization used to sample and render the wave field. It does not determine the underlying wave propagation, which is calculated from the source responses.

The app accepts 51 to 801 grid nodes per side. It requires at least 6 grid intervals per selected wavelength parameter and limits the physical domain width to 26 such wavelengths, within a 0.005–2.4 m domain range. Camera zoom can inspect a smaller region without changing the physical experiment. These are supported configuration limits, not a claim that every visual derivative is equally accurate at the coarsest grid. The Gaussian source contains a spectrum of wavelengths, so convergence must be judged for the quantity being inspected.

Grid sizes count nodes, including both boundaries. Thus 51 nodes give 50 intervals, and 201 nodes give 200 intervals. Odd node counts include the center of the domain. The 51-node option provides a coarse display; it does not reduce the underlying physical response to a 51-node propagation calculation.

Use at least 24 intervals per wavelength for visual inspection, and compare with a finer grid when slopes or small features matter. The default 201-node grid has about 15.4 intervals per wavelength in the default gravity setup and 30.8 in the balanced and capillary setups. At 25 mm in the default domain, it has about 7.7; increase the grid resolution for a smoother representation. The 51-node option requires a longer wavelength or smaller domain. A study covering three wavelengths, water and both silicone presets, and both source modes found approximately second-order convergence of the reconstructed surface elevation and finite-difference slope when the sampling density doubled from 12 to 24 to 48 intervals per wavelength. This does not make the minimum grid a precision guarantee. Physical point samples and the triangular displayed surface have different approximation errors.

Precomputed animation and performance

For fixed fluid properties and source shape, let K(r, a) be the radial response per unit pressure strength. Translation invariance and linearity give the unbounded field as

Sources with the same fluid, wavelength parameter, and forcing mode share the same K. Position changes its spatial argument; start time changes its age; pressure strength supplies the scalar J. Preparing this response once therefore removes repeated physical integrations from both input handling and rendering. The image groups apply the same principle to partial reflections, and cosine modes provide the corresponding representation for ideal walls. Here K includes the selected forcing history, viscous evolution, and the documented finite-duration envelope.

For grid-aligned sources in open water, the app prepares the response at every distinct distance on the display grid. The same distance occurs at many grid points and for many source locations, so these values can be shared. Placing a new source changes its lookup indices; it does not require solving another propagation problem. Their elevations are superimposed.

Preparation applies the existing radial Hermite interpolation to the complete elevation and velocity tables. When a source is accepted, its translated elevation history is added to a rolling sequence. Each source configuration has a sequence sampled fourteen times per settling time scale for open water and ideal walls, or sixteen times for partial reflections. It changes the display approximation, not the physical response tables or the source timestamps.

Ideal reflecting walls use the same approach for their cosine coefficients. One cosine reconstruction then produces the combined surface for each retained source configuration. Reflections do not require a new propagation calculation on every frame. Partial reflections contract each translated image basis with its temporal coefficients at input and accumulate the resulting surface elevations. Their onset corrections reuse the physical source maps. Five endpoint samples per source suffice to remove its remaining interpolation contribution after retirement, without retaining its full spatial map in the animation sequence.

The rolling sequence occupies a fixed array whose expired columns are reused. Advancing playback changes its logical indices rather than shifting the stored fields. For sequences that fit the temporary-storage budget, source insertion aligns the precomputed time profiles and adds the translated field in one array operation. Larger sequences use bounded blocks. Both paths perform the same superposition without introducing another approximation to the wave response.

The renderer updates existing surface coordinates, rain graphics, and text. Spectral integration, spatial-map construction, source translation, and the addition of complete source sequences occur outside ordinary frame sampling. Elapsed-time interpolation remains necessary: using a fixed frame index would make wave speed depend on the computer's rendering rate.

A MATLAB timer schedules each frame after the preceding callback finishes. The callback updates the retained graphics and processes pending interaction with drawnow. After a slow frame, the next update samples the current simulation time rather than queuing intermediate frames. Rendering cadence therefore changes the number of displayed samples, while the physical timestamps remain determined by elapsed time and the playback multiplier.

Manual and Auto-click source insertion preserve the running timer. An operation guard prevents a frame from reading an incomplete update. The simulation clock excludes input preparation, the associated bottom panel and audio updates, and the wait for the first complete frame containing the input. A source accepted during drawnow retains this exclusion until the following frame. This pause applies to the complete wave field; subsequent frames follow elapsed time. Configuration changes suspend timer delivery when they occur outside an active frame callback; their preparation time is excluded from the simulation clock. Pause stops the playback timer, and Resume restores its previous running state. A calm, unpaused surface retains normal frame scheduling.

Precomputed display profiles and their accumulated elevations use single precision. For open water and ideal reflections, snapshot uses finer response tables without the accumulated display sequence. Partial reflections use the precomputed spatial and temporal factors described above. Direct lattice comparisons, before the additional time-sequence interpolation, across the gravity, balanced, and capillary water setups included centered, corner, and off-center sources, followed by 120 staggered sources on a 101-node grid. Each error was normalized by the largest absolute elevation in the corresponding double-precision field. The largest normalized difference was below 7.5 × 10⁻⁷.

The accumulated animation introduces a separate temporal approximation. Tests with five independently timed impacts on a 101×101 grid, at 50 and 5 mm in water with open water and ideal walls, sampled onset, propagation, interference, and final settling. The largest absolute elevation difference from the snapshot calculation was below 1.16 × 10⁻³ of the reference sequence's largest absolute elevation. Source changes and resampling from 101 to 201 nodes also preserved the physical state. These are sampled verification results, not universal error bounds or experimental validation. Off-grid open-water sources and configurations outside the animation-memory budget use direct interpolation of the precomputed responses. Small-disturbance loading

The linear approximation requires small surface slopes (MIT). This requirement is asymptotic: it does not specify a universal numerical cutoff or guarantee a fixed nonlinear error at a particular slope.

An input is accepted only when the upper bound on the magnitude of the combined horizontal surface gradient does not exceed 0.1 throughout the remaining motion. The criterion includes active waves and inputs reserved by pending rain bursts.

The criterion corresponds to a maximum surface inclination of 5.71°. For a sinusoidal wave, this is an amplitude-to-wavelength ratio of approximately 0.0159, or a crest-to-trough height of 0.0318 wavelengths. For a monochromatic deep-water gravity wave, the second-order Stokes harmonic has amplitude ka/2 relative to the fundamental: 5% at ka = 0.1. This provides a weakly nonlinear reference scale for the operating criterion (Toffoli et al., 2007, Section 2a). Gravity-capillary interactions also depend on the spectrum and elapsed time, including resonant interactions between wave components (Akers & Nicholls, 2013).

Admission uses a hierarchy of bounds. Sums of individual gradient magnitudes provide inexpensive sufficient conditions. Where necessary, spatial and temporal intervals retain source locations and scheduled ages; the ideal-wall calculation also retains signed gradient components before bounding their combined magnitude. Every accepted bound covers complete intervals between the numerical samples. A rejected bound may exceed the actual maximum slope because its component extrema do not necessarily occur together.

In open water, the slope estimate combines a modal-energy bound, the spatial future envelope, and a Bernstein bound for the response-table interpolant, with a stated interpolation allowance. The same small-disturbance criterion applies to manual input and rain. With finite settling, source validation covers the complete active lifetime. If that lifetime extends beyond the precomputed response, an analytical future envelope bounds the remaining motion. Unrestricted settling retains the infinite-future bound.

For sources with known positions, the observation domain is also divided into Cartesian cells. Each cell spans a complete interval of distances from each source. The radial interpolant's Bernstein coefficients bound the slope over that interval and the relevant tabulated ages. Summing these local bounds accounts for the separation of source positions without assuming favorable interference. The check includes the interpolation allowance, omitted-wave bounds, and any motion beyond the tabulated time range. These bounds are used only for input acceptance; they do not modify the superimposed field.

Within each source map, identical radial intervals share one range query. The required response-table columns are gathered once per temporal batch and reused across its age intervals. Sources with the same age, lifetime, and precomputed response share these queries through a sparse spatial operator. Their individual positions, pressure strengths, and reflection weights are retained, together with the roundoff allowance for every contribution.

Repeated inputs reuse a table of cell-gradient bounds for each source position. The table covers the tabulated age intervals independently of the source start time and pressure strength. Temporal refinement is applied when the combined cell bounds exceed the admissibility limit. A 128 MiB cache retains these tables and their spatial mappings. Source and grid changes invalidate the retained entries; the complete calculation remains available when the storage budget is exceeded.

A previously established future bound remains valid at later times for the same source history. Adding the complete gradient bound of each new source gives a conservative trial bound by the triangle inequality. If this bound exceeds the criterion, the combined sources are evaluated over space and time. Removing or modifying an active source requires a new calculation; expired sources contribute zero.

For partial reflections, weighted image bounds are summed at common ages before the temporal maximum is taken. Distant-image contributions remain enclosed by an absolute allowance. Ideal walls retain the signed-gradient refinement when magnitude bounds alone cannot establish admissibility. These calculations preserve the small-slope criterion and do not change the wave field.

For ideal reflecting walls, admission also uses the actual cosine coefficients of each source position. A spatial grid bounds the sums of absolute modal gradient contributions; an analytical Hessian bound covers the distance between grid points. For a finite precomputed response, a complementary envelope retains the modal phase relationships before bounding the gradient. Temporal Bernstein controls and spatial interpolation remainders enclose the periodic source response over complete cells. Four translated copies account for the source and its even reflections. Compression residuals, spectral tails, and response-interpolation allowances are included in the envelope. The envelope is precomputed with the response and reused for input checks; it does not alter the propagated wave field.

Rain positions are prepared before admission, without advancing the private random number stream until the burst is accepted. The same loading rules apply to manual and scheduled sources, including each rain burst's pressure multiplier. Reservations retain their positions, multipliers, and physical start times. Changes to pressure strength or playback rate are checked against the complete pending schedule before acceptance. An accepted burst does not repeat the admission calculation for each arriving drop. The four-cycle forcing uses a conservative forcing-integral bound and may require a lower pressure strength to accept a complete reflected rain burst.

For ideal walls, the app also prepares the modal decay bounds used by this check. It takes the preceding sample of each nonincreasing bound, at eight samples per settling time scale. This preserves a conservative loading estimate without reevaluating modal exponentials for every existing source on each click. The lookup affects input admission, not the wave response.

When the sum of stored, all-time slope bounds already fits within the loading limit, the app accepts an input without reevaluating the remaining motion of every existing source. Otherwise it evaluates the age-dependent bounds. Partial reflections retain each image's coefficient and source age. Dominant image contributions use radial space-time envelopes; absolute future bounds cover the remaining images and the smooth long-wave correction. This grouping reduces the admission work without removing reflected contributions from the wave calculation.

Accepted source amplitudes are not clipped or normalized after superposition.

A separate second-order inviscid calculation provides a reference for the weakly nonlinear response of two twenty-impact water experiments. Both use 50-mm characteristic wavelength, 0.10 Pa s per impact, a 0.65-m domain, ideal reflecting walls, and 0.0125-s spacing between physical input times. The rain seeds are 20261021 and 1, with source positions on a 201×201 grid. The maximum first-order slopes are 0.0354 and 0.0546. Over 1.8 s, the ratios of peak absolute second-order elevation to peak absolute first-order elevation are approximately 1.22% and 2.15%, respectively. The calculation uses 512 Fourier nodes per direction on the even, 1.3-m periodic extension, with quadratic dealiasing. For the first experiment, halving the integration step from 1 ms to 0.5 ms changes the ratio by less than 0.001 percentage points. These comparisons assess the leading inviscid nonlinear correction for the stated inputs; they do not establish a uniform error bound for viscous fluids, other spectra, or longer forcing sequences.

Verification and interpretation

The numerical verification compares independently refined real-axis integrals with the late evaluation, checks modal initial conditions and limiting behavior, and checks future-motion estimates against sampled responses. Interpolation, grid sampling, timing, and app interactions require separate checks because agreement of the modal equations alone does not verify the complete application.

Numerical agreement is not experimental validation. The principal physical restrictions remain linear disturbances, infinite depth, and a homogeneous Newtonian fluid. See Physics and Materials for the model assumptions and material properties.
