Physics of ripples

Ripple Simulator / Physics

Ripple Simulator models small-amplitude gravity–capillary waves on a homogeneous, incompressible Newtonian fluid of infinite depth. A source supplies a localized excitation, and the response propagates according to the governing equations below. Gravity and surface tension restore the displaced surface, and viscosity dissipates the wave energy. The full viscous response is obtained by solving the linearized Navier–Stokes and continuity equations subject to the linearized kinematic and dynamic free-surface boundary conditions. For the definitions of the terms used below, see the Glossary.

The following table lists the physical quantities used in the model and their units in the International System of Units (SI):

Governing equations

The model is based on small-amplitude wave theory (Airy, 1845). It assumes small wave amplitudes relative to the wavelengths and small free-surface slopes. The governing equations are therefore linearized, retaining first-order terms in the surface displacement and fluid velocity. For a sinusoidal component of amplitude a and wavenumber k, the maximum slope is ka.

The tangential and normal stress conditions are the dynamic free-surface boundary conditions. The subscript h denotes horizontal components or derivatives. Velocity and pressure disturbances decay with depth. The fluid is initially at rest before the applied excitation. Positive external pressure acts downward.

Linearization about a flat, stationary surface omits nonlinear advection and higher-order free-surface geometry. The viscous terms remain in the momentum equation and surface stress conditions.

The pressure input does not add fluid. Its zero-wavenumber elevation response is zero, so it does not impose a uniform change in the level of the unbounded surface. The mean elevation within the visible area can still vary as waves enter and leave that region. Subtracting that local mean would change the physical response and is not part of the calculation. For perfectly reflecting boundaries, the zero cosine mode is absent from elevation, so the mean over the entire domain remains zero.

Gravity supplies the term proportional to k. Surface tension supplies the term proportional to k cubed. Surface tension is a restoring mechanism; it is not the damping coefficient.

The two restoring terms are equal at the gravity–capillary crossover wavelength (Falcon & Mordant, 2022):

For clean water at 20 °C, this is approximately 17.1 mm. Waves much longer than this are predominantly gravity waves. Much shorter waves are predominantly capillary waves. The model includes both restoring terms at every wavelength. The capillary length, √(σ/(ρg)), is approximately 2.7 mm; the crossover wavelength is 2π times that length. The 50-mm water experiment is predominantly gravity driven at its characteristic wavenumber, whereas the 5-mm experiment is predominantly capillary driven. Both exhibit dispersion.

The everyday word ripple does not identify a single physical regime. A pond disturbance contains a spectrum of wavelengths, and its gravity and capillary components can behave differently. The small, short waves are often strongly influenced by surface tension. The initial cavity and splash from a falling stone lie outside this linear model.

Wave dispersion

Different wavelengths travel at different speeds. Phase velocity describes a moving crest; group velocity describes the propagation of a narrow wave packet:

In the deep-water gravity limit, group velocity is half the phase velocity. In the capillary limit, group velocity is one and a half times the phase velocity. These limits explain why a packet's crests and its envelope do not necessarily move together. They do not describe every feature of a broad, viscous disturbance with one constant propagation speed.

The 50-mm component completes approximately 5.91 oscillations per second. These are physical time and distance scales; playback speed changes the rate at which they are displayed. The complete viscous response also includes the attenuation and frequency effects described below.

Each source is reconstructed from its wavenumber spectrum. The reconstruction accounts for wave dispersion, geometric spreading, and viscous dissipation.

Small elevations can produce visible rings by changing the surface slope and the reflected light. Measurements of drop-generated waves give a characteristic wavelength of approximately 31 mm and elevations of order 0.05–0.1 mm near a radius of 130 mm, approximately 0.8 s after impact (Craeye et al., 1999). The optical measurements distinguish the outgoing ring waves from the initial crater, crown, and subsequent water jet. These measurements provide experimental scales for ring waves; the Gaussian source parameters specify a localized pressure load rather than a drop size and fall height.

Wave-height measurements also depend on the excitation. In wind-driven tank experiments, reported crest-to-trough heights range from 0.06 to 8.8 mm for wind speeds of 2.2–7.6 m/s (Bobb et al., 1979). Those conditions cover a broad range of surface motion and do not define a universal capillary-wave amplitude. Optical methods also measure capillary wave dispersion and spatial decay, including the effects of surface films (Slavchov et al., 2021).

Constitutive model and rheology

Here, Tᵥ is the viscous stress tensor, D is the rate-of-deformation tensor, and μ is constant throughout each experiment. This Newtonian constitutive law gives the viscous term ν∇²u in the incompressible momentum equation. In simple shear, it reduces to shear stress equal to viscosity times shear rate (IUPAC).

The honey and compound-chocolate presets use constant-viscosity approximations supported by specified flow measurements. Their material descriptions distinguish measured conditions from extrapolation to weak surface motion. Small amplitude justifies linearizing the surface equations; it does not, by itself, establish Newtonian rheology.

Shear thinning, yield stress, thixotropy, and viscoelastic stress memory are not represented. With shear-dependent viscosity or yielding, the constitutive response generally depends on the combined flow, so adding fixed single-source responses would not solve that nonlinear problem. Linear viscoelastic models can also admit superposition, but require a different stress law and response kernel. These distinctions matter particularly for chocolate formulations with measurable yield stress or strong low-shear viscosity variation (TA Instruments).

Viscosity and transient motion

The underlying equations are the linearized incompressible Navier–Stokes and continuity equations, subject to the linearized kinematic and dynamic boundary conditions at the free surface. The response includes the surface-wave modes and the transient contribution from vorticity diffusion.

For one wavenumber, define the inviscid frequency ω₀, dimensionless viscosity ε = νk²/ω₀, and dimensionless Laplace variable z. With dimensionless time τ = ω₀t, the response satisfies hε(0) = 0 and hε′(0) = 1, where the derivative is with respect to τ. Its Laplace transform is

The square root uses the branch consistent with decay into the fluid. The surface-wave modes correspond to poles of the Laplace transform, where its denominator vanishes. Their real and imaginary parts determine modal decay and oscillation, respectively. The inverse transform also includes the branch contribution from vorticity diffusion.

As viscosity tends to zero, the transfer function tends to 1/(z² + 1), whose inverse is sin(τ). This limit supplies the inviscid response; the formula containing z/ε is not evaluated by substituting ε = 0 directly.

Why a ripple becomes less visible

Several effects contribute, with different meanings:

  • Viscous dissipation: viscous stresses within the deforming fluid convert wave mechanical energy into heat.
  • Geometric spreading: a localized disturbance distributes its motion over a growing region.
  • Wave dispersion: wavelengths separate and a packet changes shape.
  • Radiation from the window: waves travel beyond the visible area.

The last three effects are not additional viscosity. They must not be counted as arbitrary damping. Radiation carries energy beyond the visible area. Perfectly reflecting walls return waves into the observation domain. Viscous dissipation remains active in both cases. The finite-duration envelope described below applies equally to both.

Wave reflection

Perfectly reflecting boundaries are modeled as impermeable, free-slip vertical walls: fluid cannot flow through them, and tangential viscous stress at each wall is zero. The free surface meets the walls at 90 degrees. Cosine modes enforce zero wall-normal surface slope; their wavenumbers enter the same gravity-capillary and full-viscosity response used for open water.

The boundary model excludes no-slip wall layers, contact-angle hysteresis, dynamic wetting, and bottom friction. The slipping, 90-degree contact-line idealization also appears in an inviscid rectangular-container model (Daqaq et al., 2017).

The following views show a localized impulse in water at 20 °C, with a 50-mm characteristic wavelength, a pressure strength of 100 mPa·s, and a 100% reflection coefficient. The 300 × 300 mm domain is sampled on an 801 × 801 grid. Both views show the field at 0.50 s.

Partial wave reflection

Experiment > Wave reflection > Reflection coefficient selects the reflected amplitude fraction R, from 0 to 1. For an individual incident wave component, the corresponding reflected energy fraction is R². Thus 50% amplitude reflection corresponds to 25% reflected energy. Viscosity acts throughout the motion; changing R does not change the fluid's viscosity.

For intermediate coefficients, the app superimposes mirrored copies of the open-water response. A copy associated with N wall encounters has weight R to the power N. The copies retain the original source age, spectrum, and viscous evolution. A reflection does not create a fresh, undamped ripple.

This model prescribes a real, frequency-independent coefficient and specular reflection without a phase reversal. It represents reduced returning wave amplitude, without resolving whether the missing energy is transmitted into another region or dissipated in a boundary. It is not a viscous wall-layer or wall-impedance calculation. For R < 1, the boundary is not the impermeable free-slip boundary used by the 100% model, and the mean elevation within the domain does not necessarily remain zero. At 0%, propagation is the open-water response.

The reflection diagrams and equations show how mirrored sources represent single, corner, and repeated reflections. The numerical methods describe their precomputed representation, convergence checks, and supported spatial range.

Switching boundaries clears existing waves and scheduled rain. A surface elevation alone is insufficient to transfer the fluid's velocity and vorticity into a different boundary problem. Pressure strength, material, physical size, display settings, and pause state are preserved.

Depth

For an inviscid fluid of finite depth h, the restoring relation contains the factor tanh(kh), which approaches one in the deep-water limit. A finite-depth viscous model requires a bottom boundary condition and can include bottom boundary-layer losses. The model assumes infinite depth.

Height scale and simulation time

The numerical field is stored and sampled in meters. Vertical exaggeration multiplies only the rendered elevation; it does not change the forcing, wave frequency, viscosity, or propagation. The displayed factor is the total Cartesian vertical exaggeration, including the axes aspect ratio.

Playback rate specifies simulation seconds per second of real time. Slowing playback makes fast waves easier to inspect without changing the physical equations. The simulation pauses while a new wave response is calculated. During playback, wave motion follows elapsed time rather than advancing by a fixed amount per frame.

The displayed crest elevation is the maximum sampled surface elevation relative to the still water level. Trough depth is the greatest depth below the still water level, reported as a nonnegative value. Wave height is the difference between the maximum and minimum sampled elevations. Opposite extrema are equal in magnitude for a single sinusoid, but a localized or superimposed wave field does not necessarily have that symmetry. Its largest crest and deepest trough can also occur in different parts of the domain. These readings are in physical units and do not include vertical exaggeration.

Model assumptions and numerical approximations

The model assumes a clean interface, negligible air inertia and stress, no background current, and small surface slopes. It does not resolve an impacting object, splashes, breaking waves, bubbles, surfactant films, or a container's bottom or dynamic wetting. By default, the model represents the fluid's unbounded surface. Perfectly reflecting boundaries use ideal free-slip vertical walls and a freely moving contact line with a fixed 90-degree angle. Lower reflection coefficients use the partial-reflection approximation described in Wave reflection.

The simulator's loading criterion bounds the slope of the combined disturbance; its numerical cutoff is an operating choice, not a universal threshold established by linear theory.

Finite-amplitude limiting waves belong to a different regime. Crapper's exact progressive pure-capillary family reaches a limiting crest-to-trough height ratio H/λ ≈ 0.730. Here H is the full wave height, not the sinusoidal amplitude a. This nonlinear geometric limit does not define the range of a linear approximation (Crapper, 1957).

The linear, clean-interface, infinite-depth equations describe the underlying response. Their viscous tails generally approach equilibrium asymptotically. To provide a definite end to each disturbance, the app applies a separate finite-duration envelope. This is an explicit model approximation, not a material property or an exact consequence of viscosity.

Source definitions

A click in open water applies a circular Gaussian excitation centered at its physical position. Automatic rain calls the same source operation at twenty scheduled positions, with the same pressure strength by default. A separate multiplier adjusts the pressure strength for rain without changing the source shape or propagation model.

Characteristic wavelength

The source's characteristic wavelength λ defines its Gaussian width s and reference wavenumber k₀:

A localized ripple contains a spectrum of wavelengths. Their different propagation speeds produce dispersion and varying ring spacing.

In Pressure impulse mode, this parameter controls the spatial footprint of the applied impulse. There is no imposed oscillation frequency. Each wavenumber evolves according to the fluid's gravity-capillary and viscous response. The characteristic wavelength is a source parameter, not a material property or a single wavelength shared by all the rings.

Changing the source wavelength affects subsequent clicks. Existing waves keep their original source definition and physical age. A resolution change samples those same waves on another grid; it does not retune their wavelength.

Pressure impulse

For an individual Gaussian source in Pressure impulse mode, the time-integrated applied pressure is

The pressure impulse has its maximum value J, in Pa·s, at the source center. Positive applied pressure acts downward. The idealized impulse changes the fluid's velocity instantaneously; surface elevation is continuous at the input time. This initial-value formulation follows the pressure-impulse treatment (Naugol'nykh et al., 1993). Gaussian impulsive velocity potentials have also been studied in nonlinear inviscid flow (Tjan & Phillips, 2007). Here the subsequent motion follows the linear viscous equations.

A new impulse contributes zero elevation at its exact start, including when it is added to an existing wave field. The center initially moves downward; a surrounding upward response accompanies the displacement of fluid. The initial velocity distribution follows from the Gaussian pressure impulse and incompressibility.

This follows from the Gaussian source spectrum and the unit initial slope of the viscous impulse response. The velocity has units m/s; density, source width, and pressure impulse determine its initial value.

The central elevation is a transient superposition, not a single periodic oscillation. Each wavenumber has its own gravity–capillary response, so successive extrema at a fixed location are not necessarily equally spaced. A sinusoid starting at equilibrium with downward velocity reaches its first trough after a quarter period; the following crest occurs half a period later. For water at 20 °C with a 50-mm characteristic wavelength and J = 0.10 Pa·s, the calculated center reaches its first trough at approximately 0.032 s. The inviscid reference period at that wavelength is approximately 0.169 s; it does not define the period of the complete ripple. The larger initial depression and smaller outward rings also occur in the inviscid solution as the disturbance spreads radially and its components disperse.

The pressure-impulse approximation describes the generation of gravity–capillary ring waves by localized impacts (Le Méhauté, 1988). In the linear model, the surface displacement is small relative to its horizontal variation scale. Comparing a source with a drop impact requires both the spatial footprint and the transferred impulse; the drop diameter alone does not specify J. In drop-generated ring-wave models, the effective footprint can extend beyond the drop itself. Comparisons of a momentum-based source with measured rings use an enlarged impact footprint and a generation delay associated with the collapsing stalk (Contreras & Plant, 2006). Those fitted impact parameters are distinct from the fluid properties.

Four-cycle wavemaker

In Four-cycle wavemaker mode, the Gaussian pressure oscillates for four reference periods with a smooth envelope:

Here τ = ω₀t, measured from that click. The forcing frequency ω₀ is calculated from the inviscid gravity-capillary wave dispersion relation at k₀ = 2π/λ. The characteristic wavelength therefore sets both the Gaussian footprint and the reference forcing frequency in this mode. The applied pressure is zero outside the stated interval. This mode is a finite-duration wavemaker, not an instantaneous impact. Its completed response continues to propagate and dissipate.

The surface begins at equilibrium with zero velocity. The first pressure stroke acts downward. The envelope increases smoothly from zero, so the first oscillation is weaker than the central cycles.

Oscillating surface pressure can excite both gravity and capillary waves. Experiments using periodic radiation pressure demonstrate outward propagation of capillary waves (Sakai et al., 2003). The four-cycle Gaussian forcing specifies the spatial footprint and duration of the excitation; the fluid properties determine its dispersive response.

For the wavemaker, J is a pressure-time scale in Pa·s. The coefficient P₀ = Jω₀ has units Pa and sets the pressure amplitude before the envelope is applied. The signed pressure integral over all four cycles is zero.

The Gaussian footprint decreases with distance from the source center. The wavemaker combines that footprint with four alternating pressure cycles; the dashed curves show the temporal envelope. Positive pressure acts downward. Both profiles are normalized by their defining source scales.

Pressure strength

The Pressure strength in the menu, slider, and bottom panel specifies the common coefficient J in the two pressure histories above, expressed in mPa·s. Its physical interpretation depends on the excitation method:

Excitation method Physical interpretation of J
Pressure impulse The time-integrated pressure at the center of an individual Gaussian source
Four-cycle wavemaker The pressure amplitude coefficient divided by the reference angular frequency, J = P₀/ω₀

The common coefficient scales the applied pressure and the resulting wave field. For fixed fluid properties, source width, and excitation method, the surface displacement is directly proportional to J. Equal values of J across the two methods do not imply equal transferred energies or wave amplitudes, which also depend on the pressure history.

The characteristic elevation scale A₀ = J k₀/(ρ ω₀) relates the pressure loading to the fluid and source scale. It is a normalization scale, not the maximum elevation of every resulting ripple.

For water at 20 °C, J = 0.10 Pa·s and λ = 50 mm give A₀ ≈ 0.339 mm and A₀/λ ≈ 0.00678. The balanced and capillary setups preserve this dimensionless loading while changing the physical scale. The actual crest, trough, and surface slope follow from the complete response.

See Source controls for the pressure strength presets and the Auto-click and rain multipliers.

Physical scales and drop impacts

Drop momentum provides a useful comparison for the integrated impulse I. A measured 2.8-mm water drop falling 5.5 m has an incident kinetic energy of approximately 0.0003 J (Craeye et al., 1999, p. 328). Using a drop density of 1000 kg/m³ gives a mass of approximately 11.5 mg and momentum of 83.0 µN·s:

For a 50-mm characteristic wavelength, s ≈ 7.96 mm and the equal-impulse Gaussian has J ≈ 0.209 Pa·s. This strength is calculated from the reported drop size and kinetic energy; it is not a measured pressure history.

At J = 0.10 Pa·s, the Gaussian supplies 39.8 µN·s of total impulse. The linear viscous response in unbounded water gives an early central depression of approximately 0.30 mm and an outward crest of approximately 0.055 mm beyond a radius of 25 mm. The maximum calculated surface slope is approximately 0.027. These values describe a localized, small-slope disturbance; the central depression and the outward crest occur at different positions and times.

At 0.8 s, the calculated outer crest is approximately 0.025 mm in the 100–180-mm radial interval. The measured ring elevations are approximately 0.05–0.10 mm near a radius of 130 mm at that time, with a timing uncertainty of about 0.04 s (Craeye et al., 1999). The Gaussian example therefore represents a gentler disturbance. Equal total impulse does not imply identical wave profiles: the forcing footprint, impact cavity, crown, and subsequent jet affect how drop momentum and energy enter the outgoing waves. This comparison establishes physical scales rather than a calibration of an individual drop.

Overlapping sources and rain

Within the linear model, surface elevations and velocities are superimposed. Sources can interfere constructively or destructively without changing one another's definitions. Destructive interference in elevation does not imply that the fluid has no velocity or energy.

A rain burst schedules twenty clicks at 0.05-second intervals of elapsed real time while playback runs. The first is issued immediately.

Each scheduled click uses the wavelength, pressure strength, and excitation method in effect when it occurs, together with its burst's pressure multiplier.

Capillary wave A surface wave whose restoring force is dominated by surface tension.
Characteristic wavelength The source parameter λ = 2πs, where s is the Gaussian width. It sets the pressure footprint and, for the four-cycle wavemaker, the reference forcing frequency. A localized source contains a spectrum of wavelengths.
Clean interface An interface without modeled surfactant, surface-film, or Marangoni stresses.
Deep water The infinite-depth model used here; bottom motion and bottom friction are absent.
Density, ρ Mass per volume, in kg/m³.
Dispersion Dependence of wave frequency and propagation speeds on wavenumber.
Dynamic viscosity, μ Newtonian shear-stress coefficient, in Pa s.
Gravity wave A surface wave whose restoring force is dominated by gravity.
Group velocity Rate of change of angular frequency with wavenumber; describes narrow wave-packet transport in the inviscid relation.
Hankel reconstruction Radially symmetric spectral reconstruction using Bessel functions.
Kinematic viscosity, ν Dynamic viscosity divided by density, in m²/s.
Linearization Retaining first-order disturbances around the flat, motionless state. It permits superposition.
Newtonian fluid A fluid whose deviatoric stress is proportional to strain rate with the specified constant viscosity.
Numerical rest An exactly flat represented surface with zero velocity and slopes after all source contributions end.
Observation domain The physical area sampled and displayed. By default, it shows part of the fluid's unbounded surface; optional boundaries reflect waves back into this area.
Phase velocity Angular frequency divided by wavenumber; the velocity of a constant-phase crest in a monochromatic component.
Physical time Time in the governing equations, in seconds.
Playback rate Simulation seconds per second of real time while playback is running. Time spent paused is excluded.
Pressure impulse Pressure integrated over time, in Pa s.
Radial slope Spatial derivative of elevation with distance from a source; dimensionless.
Surface elevation, η Vertical displacement relative to the still water level, in meters.
Surface tension, σ Interfacial restoring coefficient, in N/m. It is not the damping coefficient.
Height scale Display multiplier that specifies the vertical exaggeration.
Vertical exaggeration Ratio of the displayed vertical scale to the physical scale used horizontally.
Wavenumber, k 2π divided by wavelength, in rad/m.
Wavemaker The finite-duration, four-cycle imposed pressure source available alongside the impulse.
