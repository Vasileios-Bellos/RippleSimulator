Experiments

These numerical experiments examine wave dispersion, interference, reflection, and viscous dissipation within the linear gravity–capillary model. Inspect the physical quantities to distinguish changes in the wave field from changes in its presentation.

Scaled water experiments

The Experiment menu provides gravity, gravity–capillary balance, and capillary water setups. The default gravity setup uses a 50 mm characteristic wavelength in a 650 × 650 mm domain. The balanced and capillary setups span 6.5 characteristic wavelengths. Changing setup establishes new physical dimensions, source settings, and playback scaling.

Gravity and Balanced start at 0.25x playback; Capillary starts at 0.10x. Both restoring forces contribute in all three setups. The characteristic wavelength sets the source footprint and reference wavenumber; a localized disturbance contains a spectrum of wave components.

Water at 20 °C. The vertical lines mark the characteristic wavelengths of the Capillary, Balanced, and Gravity setups. Plot (a) shows the inviscid phase and group velocities. Plot (b) shows the fractions of the restoring relation supplied by gravity and surface tension. At the balanced wavelength, the contributions are equal and the phase and group velocities coincide.

See Compare physical scales for a guided comparison and Wave dispersion for the phase and group velocities.

Reflecting boundaries

Reflecting boundaries switches between an unbounded surface and a domain with reflecting boundaries. Use the Reflecting boundaries axes-toolbar button, Experiment > Wave reflection > Reflecting boundaries, or B. The button and menu checkmark show the accepted setting. Switching clears existing waves and pending rain while preserving the camera, lighting, source settings, and pause state. Reset restores open water.

Reflected waves dissipate through the same viscosity model as outgoing waves. Experiment > Wave reflection > Reflection coefficient provides 100%, 75%, 50%, 25%, and 10% amplitude presets and a Custom... value. The default is 100%. Changing the coefficient while wave reflections are enabled starts a new experiment. At 50%, each reflected wave component retains half its incident amplitude and one quarter of its incident energy.

At 100%, the walls are free-slip idealizations; wall friction and contact-line losses are excluded. Intermediate coefficients prescribe partial reflection, and 0% gives open-water propagation. All use the same finite settling envelope. See Physics for more details.

See Investigate wave reflection for an investigation of repeated wall encounters and Wave reflection geometry for the image-source construction.

Comparing different fluids

Select a fluid from Experiment > Material. Each named selection restores its fluid properties, characteristic wavelength, pressure strength, height scale, and appearance. To compare responses to identical pressure impulses, select Pressure impulse and restore the same characteristic wavelength and pressure strength after each selection. Use the same height scale, domain, source position, reflection settings, playback rate, and elapsed time after excitation, as described in Compare different fluids.

Density, viscosity, and surface tension can all differ between materials. The material properties are documented with their measurement conditions and constitutive assumptions. To isolate viscosity, use a custom fluid while keeping the other properties constant, as described in Isolate the effect of viscosity.

Unbounded responses to a 100 mPa·s pressure impulse with a 50 mm characteristic wavelength. Both plots use the same distance and elevation scales. The curves use the full viscous response and the property sets in Materials, including the constant-viscosity approximations for honey and chocolate. Differences reflect the combined effects of density, viscosity, and surface tension; this comparison does not isolate viscosity alone.

Interpreting the experiments

Use a top view to follow wave propagation and interference across the surface, and an oblique view to inspect its shape. Camera changes leave the physical wave field unchanged. The animated elevation map and profiles in Observe the wave field follow a disturbance from excitation through settling.

Wave propagation and dispersion

A localized source excites a spectrum of wavelengths. Their different propagation and decay rates change the pattern as it spreads. The inviscid phase velocity describes a component's moving crest; the group velocity describes a narrow wave packet. These velocities do not necessarily coincide. Use the dispersion curves to relate the observed pattern to the gravity and surface-tension contributions.

Compare distances and physical times when judging propagation. Playback rate changes the rate at which simulation time passes, while camera zoom changes the apparent distance on screen. Neither changes the physical wave speeds.

Wave interference

The surface elevations and vertical velocities of overlapping sources are superimposed. Constructive interference amplifies the surface displacement; destructive interference attenuates it. Cancellation of elevation does not require cancellation of vertical velocity, so a zero crossing alone does not indicate rest. Inspect both quantities at the same simulation time, as described in Investigate wave interference.

Crest elevation and trough depth describe extrema of the combined surface. Their magnitudes are not necessarily equal, and the extrema can belong to different waves. The reported wave height spans the highest and lowest sampled elevations. See Examine the effect of spatial resolution for the field values and the effect of spatial sampling.

Viscous dissipation and geometric spreading

Viscous dissipation converts mechanical energy into heat. A decrease in local wave height can also result from geometric spreading or propagation beyond the observation window. Geometric spreading distributes the energy of outward circular waves over an increasing circumference, reducing local amplitude even without viscosity. Propagation beyond the observation window carries energy out of view.

In a strongly viscous response, some components relax without completing an oscillation. The fluid comparison above changes density and surface tension as well as viscosity, so its wave-height differences cannot be attributed to viscosity alone. The controlled comparison in Isolate the effect of viscosity keeps density, surface tension, and source loading constant. See Viscosity and transient motion and Why a ripple becomes less visible for these distinct effects.

On an unbounded surface, a small trailing response can remain after the main packet leaves the visible area. Viscosity and the numerical settling envelope have separate roles. Each source retains its viscous response through eight settling time scales, then fades smoothly to exactly zero by the tenth. This scale accounts for slow viscous relaxation as well as the inviscid reference period. See Physics for details of the settling envelope.

Wave reflection

At 100% reflection, ideal walls prevent waves from leaving the domain. The fluid's viscosity continues to dissipate the wave energy, but the ideal free-slip walls add no wall friction. Open-water waves also carry motion outside the observation window. Compare the two cases at the same elapsed time after excitation, as described in Investigate wave reflection, to distinguish transport out of view from viscous dissipation.

With partial reflection, each wall encounter applies the selected amplitude factor again. The coefficient prescribes the returning fraction without distinguishing transmission from absorption at the boundary. Viscosity continues to act between encounters. The resulting pattern combines these repeated losses with interference between the reflected waves; a change in the largest crest alone does not isolate either effect.

Both boundary settings use the same finite-duration envelope: each source is exactly zero after ten of its settling time scales. In the default gravity setup this takes approximately 6.8 seconds at the default 0.25x playback rate. Reflections retain the original source age.

See Examples for MATLAB demonstrations of these experiments and Physics for the governing equations and model assumptions.
