Materials

Density, surface tension, and viscosity have separate roles in this model. Density and surface tension set the balance between gravity and capillarity. Kinematic viscosity controls momentum diffusion and the viscous response. Changing a material starts a new experiment with the selected fluid properties.

Water

The Water preset represents pure liquid water, with properties evaluated at the selected temperature from 0 to 40 °C. At 20 °C (293.15 K) and a reference pressure of 0.1 MPa, its density is approximately 998.21 kg/m³. Density and dynamic viscosity use the correlations of the International Association for the Properties of Water and Steam (IAPWS) at that reference pressure (IAPWS, 2011). Surface tension uses the IAPWS liquid–vapor interface correlation (IAPWS, 2014). Kinematic viscosity is calculated as dynamic viscosity divided by density.

Temperature dependence of water density, dynamic viscosity, and surface tension, calculated from the IAPWS correlations. Density and viscosity are evaluated at 0.1 MPa; surface tension describes the liquid–vapor interface. Density has a maximum near 4 °C, while dynamic viscosity and surface tension decrease over the plotted range. Each plot uses its own vertical scale.

These correlations describe a clean interface. Surfactants, contamination, temperature gradients, and surface films can change observed wave propagation and damping. The model does not represent those effects.

Olive oil

Oil > Olive oil uses a measured extra virgin olive-oil sample: density 908.7 kg/m³, dynamic viscosity 0.0741 Pa s, and surface tension 0.0319 N/m. The nominal temperature is 23 °C; density and viscosity are tabulated at 22 ± 1 °C, with at least three measurements for each reported mean. Surface tension uses the KRÜSS pendant-drop result at 23 °C. These are sample-specific measurements, not universal cooking-oil constants (Sahasrabudhe et al., 2017, Tables 2, 4, and 6).

The study checked Newtonian behavior under its measurement conditions. This preset uses the room-temperature values without a temperature correlation. Selecting it applies a golden display color.

Silicone oils

Silicone oils are synthetic silicone fluids, distinct from vegetable cooking oils. Two specified commercial grades provide viscosity comparisons. Their parameters are typical values reported by Shin-Etsu at 25 °C (Shin-Etsu, property tables).

Grade Kinematic viscosity Specific gravity, 25 °C / 4 °C Surface tension
KF-96L-5cs 5 mm²/s 0.915 19.7 mN/m
KF-96-100cs 100 mm²/s 0.965 20.9 mN/m

Density is calculated from the tabulated specific gravity using the water density at 4 °C (Shin-Etsu, specific-gravity guidance). Dynamic viscosity is then density times kinematic viscosity. The values are nominal product data, not measurements of a particular batch. No temperature interpolation or extrapolation is provided for these presets.

The simulator models these grades as Newtonian fluids. This is a constitutive model assumption for the selected low-viscosity grades. It does not extend to every silicone product or every shear rate. The manufacturer's discussion of shear-dependent viscosity concerns substantially more viscous grades.

Both grades use a neutral light color and an opaque surface by default. View > Transparency adjusts the opacity of the filled surface. The opacity setting does not apply to Wireframe or views with lighting disabled. Alpha blending is a display approximation; refraction and absorption through the fluid are not modeled.

Honey

Honey (acacia) represents a liquid acacia honey at 30 °C, using a measured viscosity of 0.27 Pa s. The study describes Newtonian flow for its honey samples over the measured conditions (Nayik et al., 2018, Table 2).

Property Value
Nominal temperature 30 °C
Dynamic viscosity 0.27 Pa s
Representative density 1420 kg/m³
Representative surface tension 0.0801 N/m

Density and surface tension are approximations from separate samples. The density lies within a reported acacia-honey range of 1398–1445 kg/m³ across moisture and temperature conditions (Meli, 2022). The surface tension is the central value measured for a different honey at 30 ± 2 °C (Tyowua et al., 2023, Table 1).

The combined properties define a representative Newtonian approximation; they are not measurements of one common sample. No temperature correlation is applied. Composition, temperature, and crystallization can give other honeys markedly different behavior. Crystals, non-Newtonian effects, and nonlinear impact cavities are excluded.

Molten chocolate

Chocolate (molten) represents a fluid compound-chocolate formulation at 60 °C. Its approximate viscosity of 0.50 Pa s is read from the temperature sweep for a 40 rpm, 10 minute refining condition, measured at a shear rate of 50 s⁻¹. The formulation contains vegetable fat and emulsifiers (Toker et al., 2017, Figure 5).

Property Value
Nominal temperature 60 °C
Density 1270 kg/m³
Dynamic viscosity Approximately 0.50 Pa s
Surface tension Approximately 0.030 N/m

Dynamic viscosity of compound chocolate as a function of temperature, measured at a shear rate of 50 s⁻¹ after refining at 40 rpm for 10 minutes. The points are digitized from Toker et al. (2017), Figure 5. The highlighted point marks the model's constant viscosity of approximately 0.50 Pa·s at 60 °C.

The formulation's approximately Newtonian behavior was measured from 0.1 to 100 s⁻¹ at 40 °C, giving 0.992 ± 0.008 Pa s and a coefficient of determination of 0.9986 (Toker et al., 2017, methods and Table 3). The temperature sweep supports the lower viscosity at 60 °C; extending constant viscosity to lower shear rates at that temperature is a modeling assumption. Small surface disturbances can fall below the measured range, so these measurements do not establish the formulation's rheology arbitrarily close to rest.

Density is a representative compiled chocolate value (Townsend & Wilson, 2016, Table 1 and Section 2). Surface tension uses a representative 30 mN/m within the range of liquid chocolate measurements at 50 °C using the Wilhelmy-plate method (Keijbets, 2009, Section 4.3.1.2 and Figure 4.1). Neither property receives a temperature correction. The complete set is a documented approximation assembled from separate sources, not measurements of one formulation at 60 °C.

Surface tension is spatially uniform in the model. Surfactant adsorption, surface-tension gradients, and the resulting Marangoni stresses are excluded; the measured equilibrium surface tension supplies the capillary restoring force.

Comparing viscosity

In Pressure impulse mode, equal pressure strength and characteristic wavelength specify the same individual Gaussian pressure impulse. The resulting surface elevation also depends on the fluid properties. For the four-cycle wavemaker, the reference frequency depends on density and surface tension, so equal pressure strength does not generally give the same pressure amplitude or forcing duration. Keep the physical domain, source position, reflection settings, and playback rate fixed when comparing materials.

Automatic height scaling makes the smaller responses of viscous fluids visible. It compares peak center displacements from equal reference pressure impulses and adjusts the display multiplier relative to water at 20 °C. The multiplier is fixed during playback and shown as Height scale. For a visual comparison of physical amplitudes, select the same manual multiplier for every material. Crest elevation, Trough depth, and Wave height always report physical quantities before display exaggeration.

As viscosity increases, some spectral components cease to oscillate and relax without completing a cycle. The linear deep-water response includes these nonoscillatory modes, oscillatory modes, and a viscous transient.

In a high-viscosity fluid, a deformed surface can return to equilibrium more slowly, while a brief pressure impulse can produce only small surface displacements. Those are different initial conditions. Greater viscosity does not imply larger ripples or more oscillations. Increasing pressure strength scales the displacement without changing the wave dispersion or damping in a given fluid. The documented finite settling envelope is an additional finite-duration model approximation, separate from viscosity. It smoothly restores each source’s calculated surface elevation and vertical velocity to zero over the settling interval.

Custom fluids

Choose Experiment > Material > Custom > Define properties... to enter density in kg/m³, dynamic viscosity in Pa s, surface tension in N/m, and the temperature in °C at which those values apply. Density and viscosity must be positive; surface tension may be zero. Kinematic viscosity is calculated as dynamic viscosity divided by density.

Enter a consistent property set for the intended fluid and temperature. The app does not infer a temperature dependence for custom data. Editing the temperature in this dialog labels the supplied measurements; it does not recalculate the other properties. Custom fluids use the same homogeneous, Newtonian, clean-interface assumptions as the other presets.
