Wave physics

Falcon, E., and Mordant, N. (2022). “Experiments in Surface Gravity–Capillary Wave Turbulence.” Annual Review of Fluid Mechanics, 54, 1–25. Published review. Section 2 gives the inviscid gravity–capillary dispersion relation and defines the crossover where gravity and surface tension contribute equally.

Sakai, K., Tachibana, K., Mitani, S., and Takagi, K. (2003). “Laser excitation of high-frequency capillary waves.” Journal of Colloid and Interface Science, 264(2), 446–451. Published paper. Theoretical and experimental study of capillary waves excited by periodic radiation pressure, including their propagation away from the forcing region.

Bobb, L. C., Ferguson, G., and Rankin, M. (1979). “Capillary wave measurements.” Applied Optics, 18(8), 1167–1171. Published paper. Wind-driven tank measurements of wave height, slope, and frequency content. The reported crest-to-trough heights depend on the imposed wind speed and are not a calibration of drop-generated ring waves.

Crapper, G. D. (1957). “An exact solution for progressive capillary waves of arbitrary amplitude.” Journal of Fluid Mechanics, 2(6), 532–540. Published paper. An exact nonlinear pure-capillary wave family. Its limiting ratio of crest-to-trough height to wavelength is distinct from the small-slope criterion used for linear disturbances.

Slavchov, R. I., Peychev, B., and Ismail, A. S. (2021). “Characterization of capillary waves: A review and a new optical method.” Physics of Fluids, 33(10), 101303. Published paper. Review of optical methods for measuring capillary wavelengths and decay, including the influence of surface elasticity. Its optical method determines relative amplitudes; it does not provide an absolute calibration for the simulator's impulsive source.

Airy, G. B. (1845). “Tides and Waves.” Encyclopaedia Metropolitana, vol. 5, pp. 241–396. Digitized original. Classical treatment of tides and small-amplitude gravity waves. Section IV.1, pp. 284–285, develops the small-disturbance approximation by neglecting terms beyond first order.

Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). “Linearized (Airy) Wave Theory.” Fluid Mechanics Modules, Water Waves. University teaching notes. Derivation of the linear gravity-wave approximation from small surface displacement and slope.

Toffoli, A., Monbaliu, J., Onorato, M., Osborne, A. R., Babanin, A. V., and Bitner-Gregersen, E. (2007). “Second-Order Theory and Setup in Surface Gravity Waves: A Comparison with Experimental Data.” Journal of Physical Oceanography, 37(11), 2726–2739. Published paper. Section 2a relates the second-order harmonic amplitude to wave steepness and water depth. The deep-water result supplies a gravity-wave reference for the small-slope operating criterion.

Akers, B., and Nicholls, D. P. (2013). “Spectral Stability of Deep Two-Dimensional Gravity-Capillary Water Waves.” Studies in Applied Mathematics, 130(2), 81–107. Published paper. Nonlinear free-surface equations, capillary curvature, and the dependence of weakly nonlinear gravity-capillary expansions on resonant interactions.

Armaroli, A., Eeltink, D., Brunetti, M., and Kasparian, J. (2018). “Viscous damping of gravity-capillary waves: Dispersion relations and nonlinear corrections.” Physical Review Fluids, 3, 124803. Published paper. Peer-reviewed analysis of viscous dispersion and the limits of common approximations. The simulator uses a linear response; it does not implement the paper's nonlinear corrections.

Denner, F. (2016). “Frequency dispersion of small-amplitude capillary waves in viscous fluids.” Physical Review E, 94, 023110. Published paper. Peer-reviewed treatment of viscous capillary-wave dispersion and the transition between oscillatory and nonoscillatory behavior.

Patibandla, R., Basak, S., Dasgupta, R., and Roy, A. (2023). “Surface and internal gravity waves on a viscous liquid layer: Initial-value problems.” International Journal of Multiphase Flow, 169, 104592. Published paper. Peer-reviewed initial-value analysis, including surface and vorticity modes. Its broader stratified and finite-depth problems are not implemented here.

Impulsive excitation and ring waves

Naugol'nykh, K. A., Puchenkov, O. V., Zosimov, V. V., and Pashin, A. E. (1993). “Surface motion induced by the interaction of pulsed laser radiation with highly absorbing dielectric fluids.” Journal of Fluid Mechanics, 250, 385–421. Published paper. Theory and experiments on pressure-impulse excitation, including linear gravity-capillary waves in weakly and strongly viscous fluids. The applied loading originates from laser recoil rather than a falling drop.

Tjan, K. K., and Phillips, W. R. C. (2007). “On impulsively generated inviscid axisymmetric surface jets, waves and drops.” Journal of Fluid Mechanics, 576, 377–403. Published paper. Gaussian impulsive velocity potentials and their nonlinear inviscid evolution. The Gaussian initial condition supports the source formulation; the nonlinear jet and droplet dynamics are outside the simulator's linear viscous model.

Craeye, C., Sobieski, P. W., Bliven, L. F., and Guissard, A. (1999). “Ring-Waves Generated by Water Drops Impacting on Water Surfaces at Rest.” IEEE Journal of Oceanic Engineering, 24(3), 323–332. Published paper. Optical measurements of ring-wave slopes, elevations, wavelengths, and energy for 2.2- and 2.8-mm drops impacting fresh and salt water.

Le Méhauté, B. (1988). “Gravity–capillary rings generated by water drops.” Journal of Fluid Mechanics, 197, 415–427. Published paper. Linear theory of dispersive gravity–capillary rings generated by impulsive loading, including viscous dissipation.

Contreras, R. F., and Plant, W. J. (2006). “Surface effect of rain on microwave backscatter from the ocean: Measurements and modeling.” Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans, 111, C08019. Published paper. Momentum-based ring-wave modeling compared with measured drop-generated profiles. The comparison uses an effective impact footprint, a generation delay, and fitted dissipation; these are distinct from molecular viscosity.

Sobieski, P., Craeye, C., and Bliven, L. F. (2009). “A relationship between rain radar reflectivity and height elevation variance of ringwaves due to the impact of rain on the sea surface.” Radio Science, 44(3). Published paper. Sections 3.1–3.3 relate measured ring-wave energy to drop momentum and distinguish individual impacts from the statistics of a rain field.

Wave reflection

Dean, R. G., and Dalrymple, R. A. (1991). Water Wave Mechanics for Engineers and Scientists. Advanced Series on Ocean Engineering, Vol. 2. World Scientific. Book. Textbook treatment of standing waves at an impermeable vertical wall and partial reflection in Sections 4.4 and 4.6. The partial-reflection discussion distinguishes reflected amplitude from phase change; this simulator uses a real, nonnegative coefficient with zero phase change.

Daqaq, M. F., Xu, Y., and Lacarbonara, W. (2017). Nonlinearity of Finite-Amplitude Sloshing in Rectangular Containers. Journal of Applied Mechanics, 84(3), 031002. DOI: 10.1115/1.4035363. Peer-reviewed study using an inviscid rectangular-container model with slipping contact lines and a 90-degree contact angle, supporting the stated contact-line idealization.

Qin, H., Shen, J., and Chen, X. (2011). “A free surface frequency domain Green function with viscous dissipation and partial reflections from side walls.” Journal of Marine Science and Application, 10(3), 259–264. Published paper. Peer-reviewed use of weighted image sources for partial side-wall reflection. The paper's frequency-domain visco-potential formulation differs from the initial-value viscous response used here.

Numerical interpolation

Berrut, J.-P., and Trefethen, L. N. (2004). “Barycentric Lagrange Interpolation.” SIAM Review, 46(3), 501–517. Published paper. The barycentric formula and first-kind Chebyshev weights support the piecewise wavenumber interpolation used to prepare reflecting-mode response tables.

Material properties

International Association for the Properties of Water and Steam (IAPWS). (2011). Supplementary Release on Properties of Liquid Water at 0.1 MPa, SR6-08(2011). Official release. Scientific reference correlations for density and viscosity at the specified pressure. The app restricts the temperature range to 0–40 °C.

International Association for the Properties of Water and Steam (IAPWS). (2014). Revised Release on Surface Tension of Ordinary Water Substance, R1-76(2014). Official release. Scientific reference correlation for the liquid–vapor surface tension.

Shin-Etsu Chemical Co., Ltd. KF-96 Silicone Fluid: Performance Test Results. Manufacturer technical data. Typical product properties for the specified silicone-fluid grades, including viscosity, specific gravity, surface tension, and qualifications concerning temperature and shear behavior.

Nayik, G. A., Dar, B. N., and Nanda, V. (2018). “Rheological behavior of high altitude Indian honey varieties as affected by temperature.” Journal of the Saudi Society of Agricultural Sciences, 17(3), 323–329. Published paper. Table 2 supplies the acacia-honey viscosity of 0.27 Pa s at 30 °C.

Meli (2022). Acacia honey technical sheet, April 7. Manufacturer's data. Reports an acacia-honey density range of 1398–1445 kg/m³ across moisture and temperature conditions. The commercial product differs from Nayik et al.'s sample; its density supports a representative approximation.

Tyowua, A. T., Echendu, A. M., and Adejo, S. O. (2023). “Influence of particle fluorination on the stability of honey foam.” Frontiers in Soft Matter, 3, 1163393. Published paper. Table 1 supplies the representative honey surface tension at 30 ± 2 °C and the strongly viscous reference fluid used in numerical verification.

Townsend, A. K., and Wilson, H. J. (2016). “The fluid dynamics of the chocolate fountain.” European Journal of Physics, 37, 015803. Published paper. Peer-reviewed comparison of Newtonian and non-Newtonian fountain models. Table 1 supplies the representative density for the molten-chocolate preset.

Keijbets, E. L. (2009). Solid mould surface characteristics in relation to chocolate adhesion and the influence of processing conditions. PhD thesis, University of Leeds. University repository. Section 4.3.1.2 and Figure 4.1 report liquid-chocolate surface tensions at 50 °C, measured with a Wilhelmy plate. These measurements support the preset's representative surface-tension approximation; they concern different formulations.

Kumbár, V., Kouřilová, V., Dufková, R., Votava, J., and Hřivna, L. (2021). “Rheological and Pipe Flow Properties of Chocolate Masses at Different Temperatures.” Foods, 10(11), 2519. Published paper. Experimental evidence of shear-dependent, thixotropic, and yield-stress behavior in molten chocolate, supporting the stated limits of a constant-viscosity model.

TA Instruments. “Evaluating the Flow Behavior of Molten Chocolate,” RH113. Manufacturer application note. Table 1 and Figure 8 illustrate shear-dependent chocolate viscosity and the limits of substituting one apparent viscosity for a complete constitutive law.

Toker, O. S., et al. (2017). “Investigating the effect of production process of ball mill refiner on some physical quality parameters of compound chocolate: response surface methodology approach.” International Journal of Food Science and Technology, 52(3), 788–799. Published paper. Figure 5 supplies the approximate compound-chocolate viscosity at 60 °C, measured at a shear rate of 50 s⁻¹. Table 3 and the methods describe the Newtonian fit over 0.1–100 s⁻¹ at 40 °C.

IUPAC. “Newtonian fluid.” Compendium of Chemical Terminology (Gold Book). Terminology reference. Defines the Newtonian relation between shear stress and velocity gradient.

Sahasrabudhe, S. N., Rodriguez-Martinez, V., O’Meara, M., and Farkas, B. E. (2017). “Density, viscosity, and surface tension of five vegetable oils at elevated temperatures: Measurement and modeling.” International Journal of Food Properties, 20(sup2), S1965–S1981. Published paper. Tables 2, 4, and 6 provide the room-temperature olive-oil properties.
